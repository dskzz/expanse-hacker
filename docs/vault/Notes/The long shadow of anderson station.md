# The Long Shadow of Anderson Station

## Prologue: Vesta — The First Failure

The Vesta Blockade was the moment the system proved it could kill without firing a shot.

Earth and Mars both believed they were communicating clearly. Vesta believed it was being ignored. In reality, none of them were talking to each other at all. The relays between them were misaligned, timing windows drifting, metadata stripped by incompatible firmware. Every message arrived late, corrupted, or not at all.

Silence became accusation. Accusation became escalation. Escalation became a blockade.

When the UNN deployed five destroyers to embargo Martian terraforming supplies, Mars interpreted the move as a provocation. When a Martian cruiser twitched its targeting solution—an automated correction, not a threat—the UNN read it as hostile intent. Within seconds, the shooting started.

The lone MCRN cruiser crippled all five UN destroyers. On the _Yasijuro_, twenty‑nine personnel were blown into space when a hull section decompressed. Half the UNN fleet was already on a hard burn toward Mars, prepared to end the colony outright before cooler heads intervened.

The battle ended quickly. The consequences did not.

Mars responded with a century‑long militarization program, delaying its terraforming by generations. Earth blamed Martian aggression. Mars blamed Earth’s blockade. The Belt blamed both.

But the truth was simpler:

**The system failed. And when communication fails, war becomes the default.**

## Fifteen Years of Drift

Everyone agreed the network needed reform. No one agreed on whose reform.

- Earth added redundancy that overloaded Belt relays.
    
- Mars added encryption that broke Earth’s routing.
    
- The Belt improvised fixes that confused both.
    

Every patch was political. Every fix was local. Every improvement made the system worse. The network became a palimpsest of incompatible assumptions and half‑implemented standards.

This was the era where the future SolNet RFCs were born—not out of vision, but out of exhaustion. Committees drafted and redrafted specifications that no one fully implemented. Trust eroded. Latency grew. Silence became normal.

This is the era Johnson later studies obsessively—the era that convinces him the system was already dying long before Anderson Station.

## Anderson Station — The Second Fracture

By the time Anderson Station declared surrender, the network was so brittle that the message simply… didn’t propagate. The pleas for negotiation vanished into the same void that had swallowed Vesta’s warnings fifteen years earlier.

Colonel Fred Johnson acted on silence because silence was all the system could deliver.

His suit feed recorded the truth: terrified civilians, desperate parents, children clinging to their families as the air fled the station. The footage was supposed to be archived, classified, forgotten.

Johnson watched it anyway. Then again. Then a hundred times.

He replayed it in the dark, punishing himself with the truth he had helped create. He tried to understand how he had become the instrument of a failure he didn’t cause.

One night—drunk, exhausted, and unable to bear the weight alone—he pushed the feed onto the public channels.

The system tried to suppress it. It failed.

The first sustainable intersolar broadcast was not a speech or a treaty. It was dead children drifting in vacuum.

**If truth can’t move, people die.**

That was the second lesson.

## Outrage, Reckoning, and the Birth of SolNet

The Belt erupted. Earth reeled. Mars demanded answers. The old network was exposed as a machine that could hide tragedy as easily as it could reveal it.

Governments had no choice: the Belt had to be included in the rebuild.

The Interplanetary Relay Oversight Council—IROC—was formed. Dawes approached Johnson quietly, not with forgiveness but with purpose. Johnson told him everything: the delays, the dropped packets, the silent relays, the way the system had been built to serve power instead of people.

IROC became the seed of the OPA.

## Johnson's Long Road

*[Added 2026-09-05, per the user's own timeline reasoning — see `docs/lore/os-lineages.md` §1's "why the gap has to be decades, not years." None of what follows contradicts anything above; it just makes the gap between Anderson and "Ten Years Later" below honest about how long rebuilding a life actually takes.]*

What the record shows as a clean line—disgrace, then IROC, then purpose—took Johnson most of a generation to actually walk.

He did not come to terms with Anderson quickly. For a long stretch after the footage first leaked, he was not a man with purpose; he was a man who had disappeared, drifting between stations that didn't ask questions, doing the kind of quiet, anonymous work that doesn't require a name attached to it. He was, by any honest account, lost.

Dawes found him during that stretch, and did not approach him immediately. He watched him for the better part of a year first—long enough to be sure the man he was looking at wasn't performing his own guilt for an audience.

The reconciliation, when it came, was not a single conversation. It was the start of a working relationship it took years to actually trust. IROC's formation gave Johnson a task, not a redemption—that came later, earned in the slow, unglamorous way reputations actually get rebuilt: real work, on real stations, over real years, until "the man responsible for Anderson" started to share space with "the man who fixed our relay." By the time Tycho Station's operators were willing to hire him outright, put him in charge of standing up new construction, and eventually hand him a contract as consequential as the *Nauvoo*, the disgraced officer of Anderson Station was a long way behind him—not forgotten, never forgotten, but no longer the whole of who he was to the people who worked with him.

None of that fits in ten years. Reckoning, the wilderness years, a year of Dawes watching before he even made contact, the reconciliation, a real rebuilt reputation, the Tycho hire, standing up multiple projects, and the *Nauvoo* nearing completion — that's a chain of a dozen sequential, multi-year phases, and the fact that Johnson is only bitterly resented for *being an Earther* by the time this story picks back up (not for the deaths themselves, which the system has mostly metabolized by then) is itself a sign of how much time has actually passed. Call it thirty-plus years, not ten.

## Mao‑Kwikowski and the New Order

Jules‑Pierre Mao presented himself as a savior of the system—a man with resources, engineers, and vision. He offered to rebuild the network with modern standards, hardened protocols, and universal redundancy.

Governments accepted. They needed him.

Mao saw opportunity.

His company embedded itself everywhere: relays, firmware, routing tables, diagnostic channels. Some of those channels were legitimate. Some were convenient. Some were never meant to be removed.

Phoebe Station received one of them.

A backwater research site. Classified. Remote. Perfect for a little extra code.

## Decades Later — The Backdoor Wakes

The backdoor slept for decades. The system evolved around it, standard by standard, until nearly everyone who remembered installing it had moved on, retired, or stopped thinking about it at all. Old scars were forgotten precisely because so much time had passed.

Then, one day, the channel woke up.

A trickle of chatter. A handshake. A ship with no return vector.

Something was moving through a pathway no one remembered building.

## Julie Mao — The Third Fracture

Julie had grown up believing her father was a hero. But she had Belter friends. Belter sympathies. She had seen the Anderson footage. She knew what silence could do.

The Razorback gave her access most people never saw—raw routing data, legacy identifiers, echoes of old decisions. She saw patterns that didn’t make sense. She saw her father’s fingerprints on systems built on the backs of dead children.

And then she saw it: a Mao‑Kwikowski ship on a flight plan to Phoebe. No return vector.

She hired the Sherpa—a quiet, brilliant Belter who understood the network’s bones. He pulled the telemetry. He traced the chatter. He found the ship’s path.

And he found the video.

Not the protomolecule. Not the experiment. Just a fragment—enough to know something was wrong. Something new. Something the system was helping to move.

He didn’t understand it. He only knew it shouldn’t exist.

Julie left on the _Scopuli_ to intercept the ship. The Sherpa stayed behind, trying to assemble a warning. The Ceres riots killed him before he could finish.

The _Scopuli_ was intercepted.

And the system continued, carrying forward the consequences of decisions made in the shadow of Vesta and Anderson Station.

The network remembered everything.

The people who understood it did not.
