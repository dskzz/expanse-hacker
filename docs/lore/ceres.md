# Ceres

Status: locked, 2026-11-24; real-science grounding, settlement history,
and the full Cole Spindle Works spin-habitat engineering/political
pass added 2026-11-28. Second full write-up in the stations pass,
after the Ganymede Condominium (`ganymede-condominium.md`). The
Belt's biggest hub, close to a de facto capital even though no single
government formally owns it — except, on paper, one does.

**LocationChain:** `BELT:CERES` — Ceres is itself the anchoring body,
no intermediate region needed, added as part of the corpus-wide
LocationChain retrofit pass (`industrial-stations.md` established
the convention).

## Real facts: the Belt's one real dwarf planet

Ceres is a genuine dwarf planet and the largest object in the entire
asteroid belt — roughly a third of the Belt's total mass sits in
Ceres alone, real hub-siting logic rather than an arbitrary choice.
Real surface gravity is tiny (~0.029g, ~0.284 m/s²), real rotation is
fast (~9 hours), and real NASA Dawn-mission data (2015–2018, the first
mission ever to orbit two separate bodies — Vesta, then Ceres — using
real ion propulsion, a legitimate ancestor technology to this corpus's
own fusion-torch drives) confirmed genuine water ice and two
specific, strange, real landmarks: **Occator Crater**, with its real
bright sodium-carbonate salt deposits pointing to a relatively recent
subsurface brine reservoir, and **Ahuna Mons**, a real ~4km cryovolcanic
dome — one of the odder landscapes in the solar system. Dawn, a
completed rather than currently-flying mission, plays the same
present-day-anchor role here that Cassini-Huygens already plays for
Saturn.

## Settlement history: one of the very first

Ceres is one of the earliest Belt settlements in the whole corpus —
Phase 1's ambition-and-rivalry corporate-charter wave (~2100–2130,
`frontier-timeline.md`), predating even VPH, already established as
"the first settled Belt bodies *after* Ceres." An early Slavic
technical elite — the same engineering culture Korolev Propulsion and
Vernadsky Systems seeded — got in on the ground floor, and over a
century-plus that population grew its own organic political culture
rather than being a recent transplant, which is exactly why Piast's
Cossack-Hetman framing (below) lands as real inherited tradition
rather than costume.

## Why nobody actually spun up the rock itself

The obvious-sounding idea — hollow out Ceres and spin the whole body
for gravity — runs into a hard physical wall, not just a hard
engineering one. Ceres's real radius is ~470 km and its real escape
velocity is only ~510 m/s. Reaching even a modest 0.3g at the
equator would need a ~42-minute rotation period and a surface speed
over double escape velocity — material at the rim would already have
left before "spinning up" meant anything. Worse, the real breakup
threshold (where spin-induced force first matches Ceres's own tiny
natural gravity, and loose surface material starts lifting off on its
own) arrives at a much gentler ~2.25-hour rotation period — a number
that lines up almost exactly with the real, observed "spin barrier"
measured across actual rubble-pile asteroids, none of which are ever
found spinning faster than about 2.2 hours for exactly this reason.
Closing that gap down to 42 minutes would mean re-engineering the
entire body into something closer to a solid pressure vessel than an
icy dwarf planet — at that point you haven't spun up an asteroid, you've
built a different, far more expensive structure that happens to be
made of one.

**The actually-correct version of the idea already existed in this
corpus.** `corporations.md`'s **Cole Spindle Works** — named for
Dandridge Cole, a real, genuinely obscure 1964 proposal predating and
arguably originating the far more famous O'Neill cylinder — never
called for spinning the asteroid itself. Cole's real design hollows
out an interior cavity and spins a much smaller, separate structure
inside it, decoupled entirely from the parent body's own rotation.
The asteroid's shell does structural and radiation-shielding work; the
actual gravity comes from spinning a drum a few kilometers across, not
fighting Ceres's own escape velocity at all.

## Two generations, not one

**First generation (~2100–2130, built alongside the founding itself):**
a modest pair of small cylinders, ~150–200m radius, operating
somewhere in the 0.3–0.7g range — gravity comfort wasn't the
priority, basic habitability was. Built as a counter-rotating pair
from the start even at this small scale, since the momentum-balance
problem doesn't wait for a colony to get rich before it matters.

**Second generation, the Cole Standard (~2220–2250):** financed and
populated by the already-established second Slavic-coded wave — the
religious-discomfort-driven emigration of mostly old-first-world and
Russian/Eastern-European population (`frontier-timeline.md`) — landing
100–150 years, four to six generations, after the original settlers.
That gap matters: the people this generation's design choices affect
aren't recent arrivals who chose low gravity, they're descendants of
people who've been adapted to it for the colony's entire existence.

**The real economics behind the Cole Standard's numbers.** For a
spin habitat at a fixed, safety-margined rotation rate, usable floor
area scales with radius while excavated volume (the real cost driver)
scales with radius squared — meaning floor space per dollar, A = 2V/r,
*always* favors a smaller radius and lower gravity, without limit.
Pure cost-minimization would have pushed toward the lowest gravity
level already treated as acceptable for permanent habitation anywhere
in this corpus — Mars's own established 0.38g, lived at permanently
with no spin correction and no special medical intervention. The Cole
Standard's actual target, **0.75g**, is a deliberate spend past that
floor, not the economically optimal choice — Ceres's planners
reused the same real geriatric-medicine logic already established for
Venus (gravity just under 1g measurably reduces long-term bone and
joint strain) and designed for a lifetime of residents aging in place,
not just the retirees that insight was first applied to. That's a
real, earned detail about Ceres specifically: wealthy enough, via the
MFN framework's Earth capital, to build past the bare minimum on
purpose.

**Dimensions, locked:** radius **670m**, chosen so that 0.75g and a
rotation rate of **~1 RPM** — the real threshold below which Coriolis
effects are essentially imperceptible even for residents with real
vestibular decline — land on the same design point simultaneously.
Length **1,500m**, a clean round figure chosen deliberately over the
slightly-closer-to-a-mile 1,600m for easier engineering subdivision.
Built as **four modules — two counter-rotating pairs** — each
individually smaller, cheaper, safer, and faster to bring online than
one giant structure would have been, consistent with Belt engineering
culture's own standing preference for proven, reusable specs over
bespoke megaprojects. Designed target: **~250,000 residents per
module** (up to ~300k under normal conditions, ~500k with
reconfiguration), for a planned total of roughly **one million** —
though the project came in under that projection (below).

**Drive design: three rings per module, not one.** A single drive
ring risks a localized fault building into real torsional stress
before anyone downstream notices; two rings (one at each end) gives
redundancy; a **third ring at the midpoint** is both a backup and a
diagnostic — if the two end rings' torque readings ever disagree, the
middle ring's own readings tell you *where* along the module a
problem is developing, catching it before it becomes structural.

## Old Ceres and New Ceres: two cultures, one station

**Old Ceres, the Twin Cans.** The original first-generation pair
(above) never stopped being exactly what they were built as — modest,
0.3–0.7g, now well over a century old and showing it: load-bearing
patchwork, deferred maintenance, air recyclers well past their rated
service life. "Tin can" is generic Belt slang for any small,
old-style spin module; **the Twin Cans**, capitalized, means
specifically this pair, Ceres's own founding habitat. Officially still
*Spindle One* and *Spindle Two* — Cole Spindle Works' own original,
utilitarian naming convention, the registry name nobody but a clerk
actually uses.

**The docks that never got modernized.** Built too small at the time
for anything like the Cole Standard's own integrated hub, each Twin
Can docks through a **shaft-and-hub** instead — a non-rotating mast
projecting from the cylinder's own spin axis, a despun coupling at its
base, a small docking hub at the tip, the crude ancestor of what the
Core Complex later did properly. Superseded for anything serious by
the Cole Standard's own hub traffic, the two shafts found a second
life instead: lightly regulated, slightly-below-notice docking for
small independent haulers and bulk traders who'd rather not queue for
a slot in the newer, better-supervised hub. That's also exactly the
kind of entry point a dock-town vice economy grows up around.

**Canward, or Downtown.** The district built up around the Twin Cans'
own docks — gambling halls, brothels, the kind of trade every real
naval or garrison town eventually grows once enough transient traffic
and loose oversight accumulate in one place (the same real pattern
already in play at Callisto's own licensed gambling core,
`callisto.md`, just unlicensed and unzoned here). Not illegal, not
hidden — accepted, even locally proud of itself, just concentrated
exactly where the people and money that want it actually are.

**Uptown, New Ceres, the Point Fives.** The Cole Standard's four
modules, by contrast, read as the respectable address — "the Point
Fives," a real local joke riffing on New York's historical Five
Points slum, inverted into a mark of status rather than its original
meaning. Locals needle Royal Warsaw (below) over the name's own fine
print: spun up to a genuine 0.75g, it technically isn't a "Point
Five" at all anymore, which hasn't stopped anyone from using the name
anyway. **The Heavies** is the broader term — Old Ceres's lower,
founders'-era gravity against New Ceres's deliberately overspent
comfort (`A = 2V/r`, above), heavier gravity as a status marker,
openly, the same inversion that makes Ceres the one place in the Belt
where "Belter body" reads as working-class rather than universal.
Palisade's actual enforcement effort here isn't a law against vice —
there isn't one — it's active, routine policing to keep Downtown's own
trade from setting up shop uptown, a zoning preference enforced at the
end of a contract rather than a statute.

**Each New Can gets a proper name, and the names tell you
something.** Cole Standard financing came overwhelmingly from Earth
capital (above), and Earth capital markets real estate the same way
it always has — a cheap unit sells better with a name like *Vista
Crown* or *Meridian Reach* stamped over the dock than a bare module
number ever could. **Royal Warsaw** is Piast's own — the name on the
ballot long before the spin-up ever made it infamous, not chosen to
match the later politics. **Golden Ife**, named for the historic
Yoruba city rather than any living figure (a deliberate choice, the
same instinct that keeps the Magnate roster split on individual
character rather than heritage, below — a can named after a modern
politician ages badly the moment that politician does something
embarrassing; a city that's stood for a thousand years doesn't),
carries New Ceres's own strongest West African-coded neighborhood
identity. **Meridian Reach** and **Vista Crown** round out the four —
no dominant heritage, no particular legend, just Earth-financed
real-estate branding doing exactly what it always does.

**The Margins.** The formal name and the street name turn out to be
the same word: the ~700m–1km of deliberately conservative, unexcavated
rock left standing between every pair of modules (already established,
above) is officially the colony's own **margin of structural safety**,
and everyone just calls the whole zone **the Margins** — stationary
rock, honestly necessary engineering, and the one part of Ceres
genuinely outside the reach of any single module's own policing. The
Core Complex's sanctioned connector tunnels and bypass tube (above)
run through it under full supervision; the Margins' own real size
means they're also plausibly riddled with older, unofficial bores —
exploratory shafts from the original settlement surveys, abandoned
utility runs, the odd hand-dug connection nobody ever filed paperwork
for — exactly the kind of half-mapped space a determined smuggling
operation between Downtown and anywhere else on Ceres would want.

**What Ceres actually has under its own rock, and what it doesn't.**
The real Ceres is a genuine C-type carbonaceous body — water ice and
hydrated, ammoniated clays make up a large fraction of its real
composition, with Occator Crater's own famous bright spots being real
sodium-carbonate salt deposits left behind by evaporated subsurface
brine (already named, above) rather than anything metallic. That's a
deliberate, useful contrast with **Psyche** (`psyche.md`,
`corporations.md`) — a genuinely metal-rich body under Oberth
Extraction's claim. Ceres was never the metals play; its own real
economic case always ran on water, volatiles, and simply being first
and biggest, not on anything buried in its own rock being especially
valuable to dig up.

**Belt University.** Modest on purpose — a working technical and
administrative college, not a flagship research institution (the
corpus's actual heavyweight research niches already belong elsewhere:
Spitzer Station's fusion work, Muller Center's radiation medicine,
`ganymede-condominium.md`). Sited in Meridian Reach's own Zone 1, it
trains the engineers, administrators, and dock officials every Belt
institution from the Commission down actually needs a steady supply
of — Ceres's own capital-city standing makes it the obvious place for
that pipeline to sit, rather than any one corporate sponsor's own
campus.

**A low-g arena, sited exactly where the gravity gets fun.** Built at
the Zone 2/3 boundary — gravity low enough for genuinely spectacular
low-g athletics, still high enough for a packed crowd to sit through a
match in comfort — home turf for whatever Ceres's own signature sport
turns out to be (left open, deliberately, for whenever a station needs
one named). A real draw in its own right, and a quieter rebuttal to
Piast's own "the poor already live fine at a third of a g" line: the
low gravity he's happy to leave Zone 3 sitting in is the same low
gravity that makes this arena worth building at all.

**A second grav-rehab destination, and a genuinely different pitch
from Venus's or Ganymede's.** Venus sells near-Earth comfort for
retirees staying conditioned; Ganymede sells immediate relief at the
gentle end of the ladder (`ganymede-condominium.md`). Ceres sells
neither — it sells the whole ladder in one place. A single cylinder's
own radial gradient already runs continuously from near-zero at the
Core Complex up to its own rim gravity, and the station's broader
spread (Old Ceres's aging 0.3–0.7g pair, New Ceres's 0.5g standard,
Royal Warsaw's 0.75g, Oduya's proposed 0.3g module, below) means a
patient can step gravity up or down in much finer, more continuous
increments than relocating between planets ever allows, without ever
leaving the station. A real, earned niche distinct from either of the
other two rungs, not a weaker copy of either.

## Inside a module: the radial zone stack

Gravity here is strictly proportional to radius (fixed rotation
rate), so climbing toward the axis is climbing down both gravity and,
deliberately, class:

| Zone | Radius | Thickness | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Premium/Admin | 670→570m | 100m | Real houses, garden apartments, boutique agriculture, government — bright, dome-lit streets |
| *buffer* | 570→550m | 20m | Structural/utility layer, doubling as a radial transit spoke |
| 2 — Mid-tier residential | 550→430m | 120m | Decent, green, apartment-dominant |
| *buffer* | 430→410m | 20m | Structural/utility/transit spoke |
| 3 — Lower-class/mixed industrial | 410→260m | 150m | Dense, cramped, packed in alongside light industry |
| *buffer* | 260→240m | 20m | Structural/utility/transit spoke |
| Core Complex | 260→0m | 260m | Docking, traffic lanes, heavy industrial/storage, near-zero-g — see below |

**Belter stories, not Earth stories.** Real developmental biology
supports a genuinely taller population raised under reduced loading —
~4.5m floor-to-floor, not Earth's typical 3–4m. Using that figure:
the theoretical climb to the axis is ~149 floors; half of whatever
the rim's own gravity is arrives at ~74 floors up; a quarter at ~112.
In practice nobody's building that tall — the gradient only matters
at a scale realistic buildings never reach, which is exactly why a
genuinely separate low-g deck (Zone 3 and the Core Complex) has to be
built deliberately rather than just "upstairs."

**Agriculture, scaled against population, not assumed.** Real
intensive hydroponic agriculture, continuous-cycle, optimized
lighting, plausibly needs ~75 m² of growing area per person for a
full diet. At ~250k residents, that's ~19 km² needed — comfortably
covered by a dedicated agricultural band running part of the module's
length at the outer rim, stacked two to three layers deep within its
own height allowance, the same math that works at the larger original
one-million-colony scale. Recreation, markets, eateries, and health
facilities are folded into the existing residential tiers rather than
needing dedicated separate cylinders — there's real room to spare even
at this modest length.

## The Core Complex: docking, traffic, and the hub

**Ship dimensions, anchored to something real:** a representative
"average" ship docking inside the hub is frigate/destroyer-scale —
~150m long, ~20m across. Anything larger (ice haulers, bulk cargo)
docks at Ceres's own established surface facilities instead, with
shuttles ferrying people and smaller cargo in — the internal hub was
never meant to handle true bulk-freighter scale.

**Two traffic lanes plus a docking lane, each ~55–60m of real
functional radius (ship width plus maneuvering clearance), plus a
genuinely generous safety margin — ~220–260m total**, which lines up
almost exactly with the already-necessary 260m boundary above. Rather
than shrinking the habitable zones to fit a separately-sized hub, the
whole inner 260m radius is one integrated **Core Complex**: docking
collars and traffic lanes nearest the center, heavy industrial and
bulk storage built right up against it (minimizing the distance cargo
travels from ship to warehouse), with real margin left over for
inter-module wiring, comms, and control systems — the hub isn't just
traffic, it's the whole module's nervous system. **Automated tug
drones**, the same real harbor-tug principle scaled to vacuum, assist
docking at both ends.

**Inter-module connector tunnels** run hub-to-hub through the rock
buffers (~700m–1km of deliberately conservative, undisturbed rock
left between adjacent cylinders, since nobody's ever actually
excavated at this scale before) — Terebra Subsurface's work again, a
fifth genuine use for that company across this corpus.

**Surface access shafts land in the buffer zones, not the hubs
directly.** A vertical shaft drilled straight down from Ceres's real
surface into the stationary rock buffer between modules, then a
short horizontal bore into the (also stationary) hub core, means the
shaft never has to interface with anything actually rotating —
cleaner and more buildable than trying to hit a moving target.
Ceres's own near-negligible gravity makes vertical boring genuinely
easier here than it would be anywhere with real gravity, not harder.

**A parallel, non-rotating bypass tube** runs alongside each module
(below the rim, if "up" is defined as outward) as an alternate
hub-to-hub route that doesn't contend with normal hub traffic. The
real trade-off: any fixed docking point on this stationary tube can
only align with the rotating structure once per rotation — at 1 RPM,
a worst-case 60-second wait, averaging ~30 seconds with one docking
port, down to ~15 seconds worst-case with four evenly spaced ports.
Against a genuine emergency's alternative — several minutes of transit
through the full hub-and-spoke-and-rail system from a distant end —
that wait is a clear win, and **zero-g emergency response stations**
along this bypass tube are worth building for exactly that reason.

## The gravity fight: Piast, Oduya, and a fight over a problem that was already solved

**The real joke underneath the whole fight: Cole Spindle Works'
engineers had already solved it, quietly, as a side effect of the
geometry.** At the original 0.5g operating point, Zone 3's own outer
edge — where most of that tier's population actually lives — already
sits at roughly **0.31g**, essentially a third of a gravity, with
nobody having had to fight for it. The compromise setting already did
the right thing. The entire political fight that follows exists only
because someone disturbs that quiet, working arrangement, not because
there was a real problem to solve.

**The population shortfall that makes disturbing it possible.** The
whole four-module project came in under its original one-million
projection — plausibly because existing low-g-adapted Belters simply
didn't move in as eagerly as planners hoped, even into the accessible
0.5g point. Any move into unfamiliar gravity carries a real physical
adaptation cost and a real identity cost, pride included, and that
reluctance is exactly what later gives Piast a conveniently plausible
"we're just consolidating underused capacity" cover story.

**Piast's decree, and he does it alone.** Hetman Folarin Piast —
genuinely multi-generational Ceres stock himself, low-g adapted like
everyone else, not an outsider imposing Earth comfort on anyone —
decrees spinning a single module up to the full 0.75g it was
engineered for anyway, and his real motive is sharper and more
cynical than simple discomfort: quiet, publicly-funded training wheels
for his own body, beginning the re-adaptation toward higher gravity
he'll need for the comfortable off-Belt retirement at Armstrong City
he's already established as wanting. Using his own people's
displacement to prepare his own eventual exit is the real story, not
his own comfort in the present —
**"Piast's Ugly Spin,"** the decree's own lasting popular nickname,
catching the double meaning (the literal rotation, and the obvious
self-serving political spin in how he defends it, below) from the day
it's announced, the same way a real political nickname calcifies out
of one specific moment and just sticks. Reckless,
not the physics-sound paired version: it breaks the counter-rotation
balance and leaves a real, continuing unbalanced gyroscopic load on
the surrounding rock, a latent structural vulnerability nobody's
actually dealt with. Publicly framed as modernization and a signal to
attract further Earth investment; actually, in real substance, new
housing stock built for immigrants who haven't arrived yet rather
than the existing population whose labor and generations-long
presence built Ceres into something worth investing in at all. His
own real cynicism surfaces directly in how he defends it: at 0.75g,
Zone 3's own gravity range (~0.29–0.46g) is close to what it always
was, so he can — and does — publicly ask what everyone's so upset
about, since "the poor already live fine at a third of a g in my own
building." That's not a defense of equality, it's a quiet assertion
that low gravity is simply the natural, acceptable condition *for the
poor specifically*, dressed up as a factual observation.

**The real, literal Berlin Wall underneath the metaphor.** A
population that's spent four to six generations adapted to lower
gravity can't simply move into the newly-spun-up module — their own
bodies were shaped by exactly the environment the upgrade erases. A
Belter family could genuinely split across the two gravity regimes
and never be able to casually visit each other again without real
physical risk, not metaphorically. Resettlement to the remaining
slower modules follows, softened only by the project's own population
shortfall meaning fewer people are actually displaced than if the
colony had filled to its original projection.

**"Slow down the drums! Point Three G!"** — call and response, the
movement's actual street-level chant, catchy and rhythmic precisely
*because* it isn't the real policy underneath it. The slogan calls for
spinning the existing modules down; taken literally, that doesn't
work without a genuinely unrealistic retrofit: since gravity is tied
to a fixed radius at a single shared rotation rate, simply spinning
the whole rim down to a real 1/3 g would push the *worst-off* part of
Zone 3 down toward ~0.13–0.2g — worse than anything residents have
now, and squarely in the range this corpus already treats as a real
developmental risk (the same concern behind Muller Center's own
reason for existing, `ganymede-condominium.md`). Actually fixing it
without that own-goal means physically moving the boundary between
tiers outward — tearing down and rebuilding Zone 1's own high ceilings
to push everyone else out and down — a massive, realistically
impossible undertaking once the structure is already built and
populated. The crowd chants the simple version because the real
version was never going to fit on a banner.

**Oduya's actual platform, underneath the chant: build new, not
retrofit.** Rather than touching anything already standing, a
genuinely feasible counter-proposal — a brand-new module, purpose-built
from the ground up for a true 0.3g at the same 1 RPM safety ceiling,
which works out to only about **298m radius**, under half the Cole
Standard's own 670m. By the same A=2V/r economics that justified
0.75g as Piast's own deliberate overspend, a smaller, lower-gravity
module is a deliberate *saving* — meaning her real platform isn't just
more principled than his, it's cheaper, a genuinely strong combined
pitch and much more consistent with "Ceres for Belters" always having
been a concrete economic plan rather than a grievance. The chant
captures the crowd; this is the plan only the people paying attention
know she's actually pushing.

**The real compromise, and the document that gets her hurt.** Oduya's
actual negotiating position isn't to fight Piast's Ugly Spin at all —
it's to let him keep it, and build her own new module as its
counter-rotating partner, which happens to also be the real fix for
the structural vulnerability his recklessness left dangling. Smart,
cheap, and solves two problems with one build. It exists as an
internal document — dry, bureaucratic, something like a *"Core Complex
Load Mitigation & Housing Expansion Proposal"* — genuinely competent
policy work, which is exactly what makes it so damaging once it stops
being internal. A hired consultant — client deliberately unclear, even
to whoever actually does the job, though ASA's own hardline wing
(already on record considering her whole approach "too compromised")
is the obvious candidate — contracts a hacker to retrieve it. Stripped
of the cost comparison, the structural-safety rationale, and the fact
that she's getting an entire new module out of it, the leak runs the
next day as **"Oduya Sells Out to Piast"** in the blunter outlets and
**"Oduya Backs Down on Piast's Ugly Spin"** in the more "respectable"
ones — both built entirely out of her own real words, both omitting
every piece of context that would make the compromise look like what
it actually is.

**A genuine three-tier physical/class spectrum results.** Frontier
Belters (true microgravity adaptation, Loadframe-dependent even
inside Ceres's own 0.5g modules), Ceres's own cylinder population
(mild adaptation at whatever operating gravity they live at — real,
measurable difference from Earth natives, nowhere near frontier
severity), and anyone actually living in Piast's spun-up 0.75g module
(closest to Earth-normal of anyone born in the Belt). Ceres, the
Belt's own capital, ends up the place "Belter bodies" are *least*
common, not most — a real, ironic inversion worth keeping sharp. A
true frontier Belter visiting the 0.75g module would plausibly need
real Loadframe-adjacent support to move comfortably there, the same
equipment they'd need on Earth itself.

## Physical structure

A real zoned layout, inherited as structure rather than reinvented —
a government/administrative core, the docks, a public-net commons,
and a rougher outer-ring industrial zone where oversight thins out
fast. Already load-bearing: Elias Voss and Imara Oduya's Ceres
Broadcast (`os-lineages.md` §1) happened here, decades before anything
above. The spin-habitat modules above sit alongside, not instead of,
Ceres's own native-gravity surface facilities and docks.

## Formal ownership: the UN, on paper

The UN holds nominal sovereignty over Ceres — consistent with Earth's
disproportionate pattern of formal paper ownership across this corpus
(the UN itself, Castellan Ratings, UNBC, Okafor-Lindgren, Hauptmann &
Voskuijlen, Bedrock, Longview are all Earth-based). What actually
keeps that ownership functional isn't a governor or an occupying
force — it's trade.

## The mechanism: a most-favored-nation framework

A real trade-law term used directly: "most favored nation" status
means non-discriminatory treatment — whatever terms you give your
best trading partner, you give everyone with MFN status. On the
surface, the UN's MFN-style trade framework with Ceres looks like
genuine partnership: Earth's money and resources keep the station
open, investment flows both ways, nobody's officially favored over
anyone else.

In practice, formally equal treatment between a vastly wealthy
economy and a much smaller one isn't actually equal — it just means
the stronger economy's capital advantage gets to operate unimpeded.
Earth corporations can simply outcapitalize local Belter competition
without needing to cheat; the agreement makes sure nothing stops them.
Same shape as Bedrock and Longview's passive-ownership effect, or
Doris's regulatory arbitrage (`corporations.md`): nothing about it is
explicitly rigged, the asymmetry does the work on its own. The same
capital asymmetry that lets Earth outcapitalize local business is the
direct ancestor of the Cole Standard's own financing — Earth money
built the comfort Belters themselves can't necessarily afford to live
in.

## The Hetman

**Hetman Folarin Piast** — elected, and real elections are held, but
the office has drifted into something closer to its actual historical
root than its ballot-box veneer suggests: real Cossack Hetmans were
genuinely elected by council, then wielded near-autocratic authority
with very little practical check on them once in power. Piast's
surname carries its own quiet irony — the real first ruling dynasty of
Poland (~960–1370), "founding dynasty" as a name worn by a man who
holds nominal supreme power and none of the genuine authority that's
supposed to come with it. (Worth noting for anyone curious: "Hetman"
is widely believed to derive from the German *Hauptmann* — the same
root as **Hauptmann & Voskuijlen** — a coincidence neither name was
chosen to set up, but a real one now sitting in the corpus either way.)

**The leash is carrot-and-stick, and both halves are already-built
mechanisms, not new invention:**
- **The stick:** personal debt to Hauptmann & Voskuijlen — not a
  corporate contract, personal leverage, the same mechanism already
  established for how H&V ends up quietly owning things it never set
  out to own (`corporations.md`, "Hidden ownership"). A compromising
  debt, or the live threat of calling it, is the actual grip.
- **The carrot:** a comfortable retirement off-Belt once his term's

**Elections are held, and more or less fixed** — Palisade Security,

## Policing: privatized, not a real government force

**Palisade Security** (Earth-based, corporate-facing, already
established in `corporations.md`) holds Ceres's actual policing
contract — keeps the profitable, visible core (government, docks,
public-net) orderly. **Redline Contractors** (Belt-based, mercenary/
paramilitary) fills the gap in the outer-ring industrial zone, where a
"legitimate" contractor doesn't bother going and somebody's going to
run security whether it's sanctioned or not.

## The Magnates

Not one isolated puppet at the top — a whole class underneath him.
Each major Earth corp with real presence on Ceres has a local
**Magnate**: nominally an independent Belter business tycoon,
publicly the face of that corp's Ceres operations, actually sponsored
by and answerable to the Earth parent the same way the Hetman answers
to Earth's broader leverage.

Deliberately mixed, and worth stating why: Piast (Slavic) being
compromised and Oduya (West African) being the genuine resistance
risks reading as an ethnic pattern rather than a personal one if left
as the only two data points on Ceres. The Magnate roster below exists
partly to break that reading — corruption and integrity both need to
cut across backgrounds, not split along one line:

- **Emeka Falana** — Okafor-Lindgren's Magnate (West African-coded).
  Fully compromised. Placed deliberately: West African heritage isn't
  a moral marker in this cast: Oduya's integrity is personal, not
  ethnic, and Falana is the proof.
- **Priya Deshmukh** — Oberth Extraction's Magnate (Indian-coded).
  Also fully compromised — and actively so, not passively: Oberth is
  a Sol Industrial Trust member whose entire position depends on
  nobody noticing how convenient its market standing is
  (`corporations.md`), so Deshmukh's real job is maintaining that
  cover, not just benefiting from it.
- **Bogdan Wysocki** — Hauptmann & Voskuijlen's Magnate
  (Slavic-coded), and the deliberate reversal: secretly sympathetic to
  Oduya's movement, not compromised at all. Two Slavic-coded figures
  holding real power on Ceres — Piast fully sold out, Wysocki quietly
  working against the same system from inside it — makes the actual
  split visible: individual choice, not heritage. Sharp irony built in
  for free: H&V holds Piast's actual blackmail leverage, which means
  the one institution with the most power over the Hetman has someone
  on the inside who could plausibly expose exactly that leverage, if
  he ever judged the risk worth taking.

## The real opposition: Imara Oduya

**"Ceres for Belters"** isn't an abstract grievance, it's a concrete
economic platform: real tariff and trade-policy autonomy, the ability
to set terms that actually favor local Belter businesses instead of
being locked into "equal" treatment that structurally favors whoever
already has more capital. The gravity fight above is now the single
sharpest, most physical instance of that same platform — not a new
grievance, the clearest possible expression of the one she's always
run on. Oduya is the only plausible figure to lead it — already
Ceres-based, already a respected Belter elder, already carrying the
exact right history (the Ceres Broadcast, decades earlier) for a
movement to have rallied around her rather than needing an invented
new leader.

**OSL** channels the push diplomatically — petitions, formal
recognition requests, the kind of thing that actually shows up in
cross-domain negotiation, consistent with its established diplomatic
character (`belt-political-structure.md`).

**ASA** considers the whole approach too compromised — working inside
a system (petitioning the same UN that holds the paper ownership) it
should be rejecting outright, consistent with its own hardline
character.
