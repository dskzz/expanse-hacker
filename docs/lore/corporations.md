# The Corporate Landscape

Status: fully rebuilt 2026-11-21. The original version of this doc
(2026-09-04) gave the "Corporate leased-compute fork" lineage
(`os-lineages.md`) four named players alongside Mao-Kwikowski as the
one dominant megacorp. Two problems with that, caught in the same
pass that fixed the Belt's one-OPA and Mars's one-homogeneous-culture
thinness: Mao-Kwikowski is Jules-Pierre Mao's actual company from the
Expanse books/show, a specific coined proper noun that needs replacing
same as MCRN and OPA did — and "one megacorp controls everything" is
itself thin worldbuilding, the same mistake as "one org speaks for the
whole Belt." Real economic dominance at this scale essentially never
comes from one company that makes everything; it comes from networks
of nominally-separate players who either genuinely compete or quietly
agree not to.

What follows is the full result of mapping that out properly: a
37-sector taxonomy loosely checked against the real GICS sector
standard, a ~46-company roster against it, home locations (Earth,
Mars, Belt, Ganymede, or a joint venture between two), and one
dominant cartel sitting on top of all of it.

## Sector taxonomy

**Resource extraction & hauling:** ore/metal mining · ice & volatiles
harvesting · fuel/propellant refining · mining-rights survey services

**Industrial & construction:** construction materials/manufacturing ·
shipbuilding & repair · real estate/habitat construction & ownership ·
robotics, automation & heavy equipment · industrial components/
capital goods

**Infrastructure:** power generation · communications hardware ·
communications carrier/network service · infrastructure/leased
compute · data centers · terraforming & atmospheric engineering

**Transport:** cargo/freight · passenger transit

**Life & health:** agriculture/food · medicine, pharma & biotech

**Financial & legal:** banking/finance · insurance · legal services &
cross-domain arbitration · credentialing & education

**Computing & consumer tech:** personal computing/consumer
electronics · business/enterprise computing · industrial/embedded
computing · data & analytics

**Information & soft power:** media, broadcast & entertainment ·
lobbying & political influence · PR & political consultancy

**Security:** weapons/defense manufacturing · private security
contracting · mercenary/paramilitary contracting

**Consumer goods & retail:** consumer staples/mass manufacture ·
retail/distribution

**Reclamation:** salvage, recycling & waste management

**Charities/nonprofits:** a different logic from the rest — not
competing for market share, split between genuine mutual-aid/disaster
relief and corporate PR/tax-shelter fronts wearing a charity's name.

## The Sol Industrial Trust

**The name is the joke, if you know the history:** "trust" is the
actual historical term of art for exactly this kind of monopolistic
combination — it's where the word "antitrust" comes from (Standard
Oil Trust, the sugar trust, the tobacco trust). To a modern reader it
sounds reassuring. To anyone who knows the history, the name is hiding
in plain sight.

**Cover story:** formed during a past supply-chain crisis as a
legitimate resilience and standards council — real precedent for
exactly this kind of cover: wartime industrial coordination boards
have formed this way more than once in actual history. It never
actually stood back down once the crisis passed.

**The deliberate echo worth keeping:** SolNet's own OPRA moment
(`os-lineages.md` §1) was a coalition that formed under crisis and
*correctly, legitimately dissolved* once the emergency passed — that's
the explicit point of that whole section. The Trust is the sinister
mirror of the same shape: a coalition that formed under a similar
pretext and never let go.

**What actually makes it dangerous:** no single member is a villain.
Each one individually just looks like the ordinary dominant player in
its own sector. The tell — if anyone could ever prove it — is that
none of them ever encroaches on another's territory, ever competes on
price across sector lines, or ever gets meaningfully undercut. Between
the four of them they hold compute, money, terraforming, and raw
materials: the four things nothing else in the system can function
without. That's distributed and deniable in a way one company never
was, and that's what makes it worse than Mao-Kwikowski ever was: proving
collusion means cross-referencing four separate companies' behavior
over years, not catching one company doing one bad thing. The puzzle
*is* the proof.

### Okafor-Lindgren — infrastructure/leased compute (Earth)

**Sells:** infrastructure and leased compute — the actual backbone
capacity other companies and domains rent rather than own. The
"Corporate leased-compute" archetype's flagship instance.

**How they make money:** the same subscription-gated trust mechanic
the whole archetype runs on (`os-lineages.md` §2–3), but at a scale
nobody else in the system can match — they were present at and
contributed heavily to the original SolNet standards effort, which is
exactly why they understand the system better than almost anyone else
alive, and exactly why nobody's afraid of them yet.

**Vibe:** the company everyone assumes is watching, because it
probably is, and because it helped write the rules it's now the best
in Sol at bending.

**Gameplay hook:** Okafor-Lindgren's footprint is everywhere — any
exploit touching leased infrastructure trust almost certainly touches
them somewhere in the chain, without ever making them the obvious
antagonist of any single incident.

### Hauptmann & Voskuijlen — banking/finance (Earth)

**Sells:** merchant banking and trade finance — large state-adjacent
ventures nobody else can underwrite at scale, resettlement schemes
very much included (`os-lineages.md`'s ~2318 Frontier Opportunity
Program entry).

**How they make money:** financing passage, contracts, and debt for
the people and institutions too large or too desperate to use ordinary
credit — and holding the paper afterward. The "volunteers" who signed
up for the Frontier Opportunity Program likely owe H&V, not the
program itself, for their passage. That's not a side detail, that's
the actual business model.

**Vibe:** old money, impeccably polite, genuinely helpful right up
until the moment the debt comes due.

**Gameplay hook:** a Hauptmann & Voskuijlen debt contract is a
different kind of record from anything electronic-provenance-based
elsewhere in the corpus — a financial obligation that outlives the
ship, the job, or the reason someone took it on, and that someone
else can inherit, forge, or erase.

### Meridiani Systems — Mars terraforming (Mars, residual Earth stake)

**Sells:** Mars atmospheric processors and terraforming
infrastructure — named for the real Meridiani Planum.

**How they make money:** a multi-generational monopoly on the one
category of infrastructure an entire planet's long-term habitability
actually depends on. Started as an Earth–Mars joint scientific venture
before MDR-side ownership consolidated; the residual Earth stake is a
real, standing lever Earth holds over Mars's own atmosphere.

**Vibe:** dynastic, patient, publicly beloved — "the company that
made Mars breathable" is a genuinely sympathetic story to the public,
which makes it a very good place to hide something.

**Gameplay hook:** control of atmospheric infrastructure is
existential leverage in a way no other utility in this corpus is —
a puzzle about Meridiani Systems is never just about data, it's about
what happens to real people if the wrong thing gets touched.

### Oberth Extraction — Belt mining (Belt)

**Sells:** Belt mining and raw-material extraction — named for Hermann
Oberth, a real, genuinely obscure-to-most-readers rocketry pioneer,
the same naming move as Slayton Construction Yards.

**How they make money:** volume, scale, and exclusive claim rights
over extraction zones nobody else can economically contest — the raw
materials every other tier-two company's physical product ultimately
traces back to.

**Vibe:** unglamorous, extractive, everywhere a rock gets cracked open
— the company Scrapshell culture resents most and depends on most, at
the same time.

**Gameplay hook:** extraction-rights records and claim-chain
provenance are a natural, physical-custody-flavored exploit surface —
a "who actually has the real claim here" puzzle, distinct from
Tanager's courier-manifest fraud.

## Earth's military-industrial decline

Doesn't fit neatly as one more independent, so it gets its own section:

**Rickover Naval Yards** (Earth — a large, sprawling complex on Luna
plus several orbital facilities, named for Hyman Rickover, the real
father of the US nuclear navy) is Earth's domestic shipbuilder and
weapons producer, and the deliberate echo of a dynamic worth keeping
from the Expanse without borrowing anything specific from it: a large,
old, institutionally entrenched navy that's visibly losing its
technical edge to a smaller, newer rival. After several decades of a
peace where the biggest military expense is pirate-hunting, and with
the UNN and MRN now operating as a joint fleet, an increasing share of
that fleet's actual *modern* armaments has quietly shifted to
Mars-sourced designs (**Calloway Munitions**, **Ashdown Munitions** —
both Mars) simply because Mars's stuff is better, not out of any
formal policy. Rickover isn't irrelevant — still huge, still the
backbone for hulls and legacy systems, still employs enormous numbers
of people — but it's losing ground on the thing that actually matters
in a fight, a real gradient rather than a flat replacement.

**The software layer tells the same story:** Rickover's control
software and drivers are genuinely error-prone and outdated, and this
isn't a new invention — it's the natural symptom of something already
established. Earthstock (`os-lineages.md` §2) is already characterized
as "textbook-correct, and over-trusting of its own PKI: designed
assuming a reachable CA." Rickover's hardware problem and Earthstock's
software problem are the same underlying story told at two different
layers, which is what makes it feel load-bearing rather than arbitrary.

**The labor hook:** Rickover's workforce watching their own navy's
weapons contracts drift to Mars while their own yards get starved of
modern work is exactly the kind of grievance **the New International**
(`earth-political-structure.md`) would organize around — a concrete
"they're letting our industry die to save money on a joint fleet"
labor fight, not abstract anti-corporate politics.

## State & public media

A different structural question from the rest, flagged separately
because news feeds aren't just a company that exists *alongside*
SolNet, they're traffic riding *on* it — which means every media
company below is a real network participant subject to the same rules
every other node is (RFC-2353's admission-fairness language applies
to a feed exactly as much as to any other traffic; feed authenticity
is a provenance/identity problem in the same family as manifest fraud
or forged credentials elsewhere in this doc). Whether feeds need their
own record type/semantics in the RFC corpus, or already fit inside
RelayAdvertisement/duty-reservation machinery that exists, is an open
mechanical design question for whenever this corpus moves from
worldbuilding into protocol design for this specific sector.

Media splits into three real tiers, not one flat list:

**Major corporate:**
- **Clarion Media** (Earth) — Sol-wide broadcast/news. Uplifting,
  trustworthy-sounding brand name doing a genuinely heavy amount of
  quiet narrative work; the Ceres Broadcast (`os-lineages.md` §1) is
  the thing institutions like Clarion exist precisely to prevent from
  happening twice. **Gameplay hook:** proving a feed was shaped, not
  just biased, is a harder puzzle than finding one forged document.
- **Driftcast Media** (Belt) — the Belt's own independent press,
  deliberate contrast to Earth-based Clarion.

**State/public broadcasters** (both lean into the same ironic-naming
logic already established for their governments):
- **UNBC** (United Nations Broadcasting Corporation, Earth) — direct
  echo of the real BBC/CBC/ABC public-broadcaster naming convention.
- **MPA** (Martian Public Access, Mars) — the state broadcaster
  quietly drops both "Democratic" and "Republic" from its own branding
  the way BBC doesn't say "United Kingdom" — a small, deliberate
  detail that the one institution supposed to perform openness the
  hardest didn't bother including the legitimizing words at all.

**Independent/pirate (long tail):** numerous, scrappy, mostly Belt-
flavored, not individually named any more than the sector-10
independents below are — a category, not an enumerated roster, unless
a specific scenario needs a standout example.

**Still open:** whether OSL/ASA want their own broadcast arm distinct
from Driftcast's independent-press framing, for symmetry with UNBC/
MPA, or whether Belt media staying more fragmented is itself the
point of contrast. Not decided.

## The independents

Roughly thirty companies genuinely competing for market share, with no
institutional reason to assume the Trust's four members are
coordinating against them.

### VARS (Voss-Achebe Relay Systems) — comms hardware (Belt)

**Sells:** physical hardware — antennas, tightbeam terminals, relay
buffers. Not infrastructure or software; the actual boxes.

**How they make money:** components ship sealed and unauditable,
throttled below their real rated capability unless a subscription is
current. A VARS-MK2 buffer's hardware genuinely supports RFC-2305's
full rated duty cycle; the firmware caps it lower unless
`subscription_current` checks out against a reachable licensing
server. See `db/components/vars-buffer-mk2.json` and
`db/hardware/relay-courier-rig-class-c.json` for the concrete content
this produces.

**Vibe:** unglamorous, everywhere, the utility company nobody likes
but everyone's hardware is stamped with. (Note: this predates Elias
Voss's reintroduction as a historical figure elsewhere in the corpus —
open call on whether the shared surname is a nice accident worth
keeping or a collision worth renaming.)

**Gameplay hook:** "degrading" a VARS component — reflashing it past
its subscription lock — is common enough Belt-wide to be an actual
verb (`os-lineages.md` §5's `/dev` entry).

### Trellis Underwriting Group — insurance (Earth)

**Sells:** "conformance bonds" — insurance that pays out if a
counterparty's node turns out non-conformant or actively malicious.

**How they make money:** actuarial risk-pricing on node behavior they
have no formal standing to score — a reputation-score system smuggled
in through insurance premiums instead of protocol fields.

**Vibe:** actuarial, smug, litigious, extremely good at sounding
reasonable.

**Gameplay hook:** a node's Trellis bond status and its actual
measured protocol behavior can disagree — the puzzle is noticing the
two records don't match.

### Tanager Freight & Custody — cargo/freight (Belt)

**Sells:** physical courier transport — the literal ship-based
store-and-forward RFC-2305 §9 gestures at.

**How they make money:** custody-chain paperwork for bundles too
large, sensitive, or urgent for RF/tightbeam, run parallel to the
electronic ledger (`RFC-2302`/`2302A`) — the two don't always
reconcile cleanly.

**Vibe:** blue-collar, freight-dock energy, ship culture.

**Gameplay hook:** manifest fraud and forged custody chains, a
physical chain-of-custody puzzle distinct from anything electronic.

### Ondine Personal Systems — personal computing (Earth)

**Sells:** wrist terminals and personal comms, the consumer end of
RFC-2395.

**How they make money:** walled-garden lock-in, funded by telemetry at
the edge of what RFC-2308's minimization doctrine tolerates.

**Vibe:** consumer-tech energy transplanted onto a Belt/inner-system
market — friendly branding over an extractive default.

**Gameplay hook:** gives SPERB a permanent, named adversary at the
personal/PNI layer.

### Lodestar Shipyards — shipbuilding integrator (Belt)

**Sells:** full-ship construction and integration — owns **Slayton
Construction Yards** as one of its facilities, run day-to-day by Elias
Voss, corporately owned by Lodestar's board.

**How they make money:** faster turnaround and lower bids than
Anchorpoint; the yard everyone *without* a joint UN/MDR contract
actually uses.

**Vibe:** scrappy, genuinely competitive, resents being treated as
second-tier by anyone who's only heard of Slayton specifically because
of the Frontier Opportunity Program.

**Gameplay hook:** the UN and the corporate backers treat Lodestar/
Slayton as a contractor to manage, not a partner to trust — and the
gap between what Voss believes he's running and what the sponsoring
powers are using it for is the actual story.

### The shipbuilding supply chain (nearby sectors, not direct competitors)

- **Anchorpoint Shipwrights** (Mars) — general-purpose rival
  integrator to Lodestar.
- **Corelli Drive Systems** (Mars) — propulsion/engines only, supplies
  both Lodestar and Anchorpoint rather than competing with either.
- **Patchwright Conversions** (Belt) — refit and conversion of
  existing hulls, the value-chain step up from Driftwood's pure scrap.
- **Skipjack Craft Co.** (Belt) — small craft and shuttles, a
  genuinely different market from interplanetary vessels.
- **Garrison Shipworks** (Mars) — military/naval specialist, the
  shipbuilding partner to Calloway and Ashdown.

### Driftwood Salvage & Reclamation — salvage (Belt)

**Sells:** salvage, reclamation, recycling — distinct from Scrapshell,
which is a *dialect/vernacular culture* (`os-lineages.md` §2), not a
company; Driftwood is an actual corporate operator Scrapshell
technicians sometimes work for and sometimes work around.

**Vibe:** nautical, opportunistic, the closest thing to a folk hero on
this whole roster.

**Gameplay hook:** salvage-claim legitimacy depends on provenance — a
forged abandonment timestamp legitimizes what's actually theft, the
mirror image of Frostline's survey-claim mechanic below.
